Disciplinary Literacy

Help pupils learn how experts read, write, think and communicate within each subject.

Core concept

What Is Disciplinary Literacy?

Disciplinary literacy recognises that every subject has its own language, conventions and ways of reading, writing, speaking and thinking.

It involves teaching pupils to

  • Read texts in ways that suit the subject
  • Recognise subject-specific text structures
  • Use technical vocabulary accurately
  • Construct explanations and arguments appropriately
  • Communicate like an expert in the discipline

Subject-specific reading

Reading Like an Expert

Expert readers use different approaches depending on the discipline. A scientist, historian and mathematician may each attend to different features of a text.

Examples

  • Scientists examine evidence, reliability, technical language and relationships between text and diagrams.
  • Historians consider provenance, context, perspective and competing interpretations.
  • Mathematicians move between prose, symbols, equations, graphs and representations.
  • Geographers combine written text with maps, data, images and spatial evidence.

Reading prompts

How do diagrams, symbols or data contribute to meaning?

What should the reader question or verify?

Try this

Model reading a short subject text aloud. Explain what you notice, where you pause, which clues you use and how you resolve confusion.

Technical language

Disciplinary Vocabulary

Pupils need more than definitions. They need repeated opportunities to hear, read, discuss, retrieve and apply subject-specific vocabulary in meaningful contexts.

Effective vocabulary teaching includes

  • Explicit explanation of meaning
  • Examples and non-examples
  • Morphology, roots, prefixes and suffixes
  • Connections between related words
  • Repeated retrieval and application
  • Use in speaking and extended writing

Vocabulary prompts

Cross-curricular language

Tier 2 Vocabulary

Tier 2 words are ambitious, high-utility words that appear across different subjects and types of text. They are often important for reading comprehension, discussion and academic writing.

Examples

  • Analyse
  • Interpret
  • Significant
  • Contrast
  • Justify
  • Consequence

Select words that

  • Appear across several subjects or contexts
  • Are important for understanding demanding texts
  • Help pupils express complex ideas precisely
  • Are unlikely to be learned through everyday conversation

Planning prompts

Which words are essential for understanding this lesson?

Which words will pupils encounter in other subjects?

How will pupils use these words in speech and writing?

When will the vocabulary be revisited?

Try this

Choose three high-value Tier 2 words from an upcoming text. Introduce them before reading, revisit them during discussion and require pupils to use them in a final written response.

Subject-specific language

Tier 3 Vocabulary

Tier 3 words are specialist terms associated with a particular subject, topic or discipline. Pupils need to understand both their precise definitions and how experts use them.

Examples

  • Science: photosynthesis, isotope, homeostasis
  • Mathematics: coefficient, congruent, gradient
  • History: suffrage, imperialism, chronology
  • Geography: urbanisation, erosion, sustainability

Teach more than a definition

  • Show the word in a meaningful subject context
  • Use diagrams, examples and non-examples
  • Explain related words and word families
  • Contrast the term with easily confused concepts
  • Require accurate use in explanations and arguments

Explicit instruction

Vocabulary Instruction

Effective vocabulary teaching combines clear explanation with repeated encounters, retrieval, discussion and purposeful application. Introducing a definition is only the beginning.

A useful teaching sequence

  1. Select a small number of high-value words.
  2. Explain each word clearly in context.
  3. Explore morphology, roots and related words.
  4. Use examples, non-examples and visual representations.
  5. Provide opportunities to speak and write using the word.
  6. Retrieve and revisit the word over time.

Ways to deepen word knowledge

  • Compare related and contrasting terms
  • Sort examples and non-examples
  • Explore prefixes, suffixes and roots
  • Use words in several different contexts
  • Ask pupils to explain connections between words

Planning prompts

Try this

Return to newly taught vocabulary several days later. Ask pupils to retrieve the meaning, use the word in a new context and connect it to another concept.

Reading Strategies

Practical classroom approaches that improve comprehension and understanding.

Collaborative comprehension

Reciprocal Reading

Reciprocal reading is a structured, discussion-based approach in which pupils use four comprehension strategies together: predicting, questioning, clarifying and summarising.

The four roles

  1. Predict: anticipate what the text may contain.
  2. Question: ask questions about meaning and ideas.
  3. Clarify: resolve unfamiliar words or confusing sections.
  4. Summarise: identify and explain the central ideas.

How to introduce it

  • Model each strategy explicitly
  • Practise one strategy at a time
  • Use short, carefully chosen texts initially
  • Assign roles during small-group reading
  • Gradually release responsibility to pupils

Useful prompts

Predict: What do you expect to learn next?

Question: What important question does this section answer?

Clarify: Which word or sentence needs further explanation?

Summarise: What is the most important idea here?

Try this

Begin with one short paragraph. Model all four strategies aloud before asking pairs to repeat the process with a second paragraph.

Detailed text analysis

Close Reading

Close reading involves examining a short, appropriately demanding text carefully and repeatedly in order to understand its meaning, structure, language and evidence.

A useful sequence

  1. Read first for overall meaning.
  2. Identify the central claim or idea.
  3. Examine significant words, phrases or representations.
  4. Explore how the text is structured.
  5. Use evidence from the text to justify interpretations.

Useful prompts

What is the central idea of this section?

Which detail is most important, and why?

Why has the author used this particular word or example?

How does this paragraph develop the argument?

Which evidence supports your interpretation?

Use with care

Close reading should focus attention on meaningful features of the text. Avoid excessive annotation or questions about trivial details that do not deepen understanding.

Accuracy and expression

Reading Fluency

Fluent reading combines accuracy, automaticity and appropriate expression. When word reading demands less effort, pupils can devote more attention to understanding the text.

Useful approaches

  • Teacher-modelled fluent reading
  • Echo reading
  • Paired or assisted reading
  • Repeated reading of short passages
  • Explicit attention to phrasing and punctuation

What to model

Where should the reader pause?

Which words need emphasis?

How does punctuation shape the meaning?

Which technical words may interrupt fluent reading?

Try this

Read a short subject-specific passage aloud while pupils follow. Then use echo reading, with pupils repeating each meaningful phrase using the same pace and expression.

Active comprehension

Comprehension Strategies

Comprehension strategies make the thinking of successful readers visible. Teachers model them explicitly and help pupils apply them until their use becomes increasingly independent.

Key strategies

  • Activating relevant background knowledge
  • Predicting
  • Questioning
  • Clarifying
  • Making inferences
  • Summarising
  • Monitoring understanding

Before, during and after reading

Before: What relevant knowledge do you already have?

During: Does this make sense? What needs clarification?

After: What are the central ideas and supporting details?

Try this

Select one strategy that closely matches the demands of the text. Model it briefly, let pupils practise it with guidance, and then ask them to explain how it supported their understanding.
