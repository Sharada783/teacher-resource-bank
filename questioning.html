Frameworks

Use these models to plan questions that increase the depth and complexity of pupils' thinking.

Essential framework

Bloom's Taxonomy

A framework for planning learning that moves pupils from remembering and understanding towards applying, analysing, evaluating and creating.

Useful for

  • Planning higher-order questions
  • Increasing the cognitive challenge of tasks
  • Writing learning objectives
  • Designing assessments and classroom activities

The six cognitive processes

  1. Remember: retrieve relevant knowledge
  2. Understand: explain ideas and concepts
  3. Apply: use knowledge in a situation
  4. Analyse: examine relationships and patterns
  5. Evaluate: make and justify judgements
  6. Create: produce or design something new

Essential framework

Webb's Depth of Knowledge (DOK)

Developed by Norman Webb, the Depth of Knowledge framework measures the cognitive complexity of a task rather than its difficulty. It helps teachers design activities that require increasingly sophisticated thinking.

Useful for

  • Planning challenging learning activities
  • Improving higher-order questioning
  • Designing assessments
  • Increasing depth rather than workload

The four DOK levels

  1. Level 1: Recall and Reproduction
  2. Level 2: Skills and Concepts
  3. Level 3: Strategic Thinking
  4. Level 4: Extended Thinking

Essential framework

SOLO Taxonomy

SOLO (Structure of the Observed Learning Outcome) helps teachers understand how pupils' thinking develops from simple recall towards increasingly connected and abstract understanding.

Useful for

  • Assessment for learning
  • Success criteria
  • Progression in thinking
  • Self and peer assessment

The five SOLO levels

  1. Pre-structural
  2. Uni-structural
  3. Multi-structural
  4. Relational
  5. Extended Abstract

Essential strategy

Socratic Questioning

Socratic questioning encourages pupils to explain, justify, challenge and refine their thinking through carefully sequenced dialogue rather than simple recall questions.

Useful for

  • Class discussion
  • Critical thinking
  • Oracy
  • Developing reasoning

Common question types

  1. Clarification
  2. Probing assumptions
  3. Examining evidence
  4. Exploring viewpoints
  5. Considering implications
  6. Reflecting on the question

Classroom Strategies

Practical approaches for improving participation, thinking time and the quality of pupil responses.

Participation strategy

Think, Pair, Share

Pupils first think independently, then rehearse and refine their ideas with a partner before contributing to a whole-class discussion.

How to use it

  1. Ask one clear and sufficiently challenging question.
  2. Give pupils silent thinking or writing time.
  3. Ask pairs to compare and improve their responses.
  4. Invite selected pupils to share with the class.

Useful for

  • Increasing participation
  • Giving pupils rehearsal time
  • Improving the quality of responses
  • Supporting pupils who need additional processing time

Try this

Ask pupils to improve their first answer after speaking to a partner: “What would you now add, change or clarify?”

Thinking-time strategy

Wait Time

Deliberate silence after asking a question gives pupils time to process, retrieve knowledge and construct a more thoughtful response.

How to use it

  1. Ask the question before naming a pupil.
  2. Pause and allow everyone to think.
  3. Use writing or partner rehearsal for more complex questions.
  4. Allow a second pause after a pupil responds before reacting.

Useful for

  • Higher-order questions
  • Improving response quality
  • Increasing the range of pupils participating
  • Reducing rushed or superficial answers

Try this

Say: “I will give everyone thinking time before I choose someone to respond.”

Inclusive questioning strategy

Cold Calling

The teacher asks a question, gives everyone time to think and then selects a pupil to answer rather than relying only on volunteers.

How to use it

  1. Establish that everyone is expected to think.
  2. Ask the question before selecting a pupil.
  3. Provide appropriate wait time or partner rehearsal.
  4. Respond supportively and help pupils develop partial answers.

Useful for

  • Checking understanding across the class
  • Making participation more inclusive
  • Reducing reliance on frequent volunteers
  • Maintaining attention and accountability

Use with care

Cold calling should feel predictable and supportive, not like a punishment. Pair it with thinking time, prompts and opportunities to return to pupils.

Assessment strategy

Hinge Questions

A hinge question is a carefully designed diagnostic question used at a key point in a lesson to decide whether pupils are ready to move on.

A strong hinge question should

  • Focus on one important concept or misconception
  • Be answerable by every pupil at the same time
  • Provide information quickly
  • Lead to a clear teaching decision

How to use it

  1. Identify the understanding pupils need before progressing.
  2. Write plausible answers linked to common misconceptions.
  3. Collect responses from the whole class simultaneously.
  4. Reteach, adapt or move forward according to the responses.

Example

Ask a multiple-choice question in which each incorrect option reveals a different misconception. Pupils can respond using mini-whiteboards, cards, fingers or an online poll.

Research and Professional Reading

Evidence, guidance and professional reading about effective classroom questioning.

Research summary

Rosenshine’s Principles of Instruction

Barak Rosenshine brought together findings from cognitive science, classroom research and studies of effective teachers to identify ten research-based principles of instruction.

Particularly relevant to questioning

  • Ask a large number of questions
  • Check the responses of all pupils
  • Probe pupils’ explanations and reasoning
  • Use questions to identify misconceptions
  • Check understanding before moving on

Questions to consider

Are all pupils being required to think?

How will I know whether the class is ready to move on?

Which follow-up question will reveal the pupil’s reasoning?

Formative assessment

Dylan Wiliam and Formative Assessment

Dylan Wiliam’s work explores how teachers can use evidence of pupils’ learning to make better decisions about what to do next. Effective questioning is central to this process.

Key ideas

  • Elicit evidence of pupils’ understanding
  • Design questions that reveal thinking, not only answers
  • Use responses to adapt subsequent teaching
  • Activate pupils as learning resources for one another
  • Support pupils to take greater ownership of learning

Questions to consider

What will pupils’ answers tell me about their understanding?

What will I do differently in response to each possible answer?

Does this question reveal reasoning, or only factual recall?

Evidence-informed guidance

EEF Guidance

The Education Endowment Foundation provides research summaries, practical recommendations and planning tools related to assessment, metacognition, pupil independence and classroom discussion.

Particularly useful for

  • Planning questions that promote reflection
  • Helping pupils plan, monitor and evaluate learning
  • Developing metacognitive talk
  • Supporting pupil independence
  • Connecting research with classroom practice

Questions to support metacognition

Plan: What strategy will you use, and why?

Monitor: How will you know whether it is working?

Evaluate: What would you change next time?
