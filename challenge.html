Planning for Greater Challenge

Approaches for increasing the depth and complexity of learning without simply increasing the quantity of work.

Curriculum principle

Depth Before Acceleration

Challenge does not always mean moving pupils on to new content. It can mean exploring important ideas with greater depth, precision, connection and independence.

What greater depth can involve

  • Explaining why an idea or method works
  • Connecting knowledge across topics
  • Identifying patterns, principles and exceptions
  • Applying learning in unfamiliar situations
  • Evaluating alternative interpretations or approaches

Planning questions

What could pupils explain, justify or prove?

How could they connect this learning to earlier knowledge?

Could the same content support a more sophisticated response?

Try this

Before adding harder content, ask pupils to improve an existing response by adding reasoning, evidence, connections or a counterexample.

Inclusive task design

Low-Floor, High-Ceiling Tasks

These tasks offer an accessible starting point while allowing pupils to pursue increasingly complex ideas, methods and generalisations.

Strong tasks often include

  • More than one possible entry point
  • Several valid approaches or representations
  • Opportunities to notice patterns and make conjectures
  • Extensions that deepen the same idea
  • Scope for explanation, justification or proof

Planning questions

Can every pupil make a meaningful start?

Where can the task lead once the first solution is found?

Does the extension deepen the thinking or merely add more examples?

Try this

Replace a tightly structured worksheet with one rich starting problem. Invite pupils to find multiple methods, test a conjecture or create a related problem of their own.

Learning habit

Productive Struggle

Productive struggle gives pupils time to grapple with worthwhile difficulty while the teacher provides carefully judged support rather than immediately removing the challenge.

Productive struggle is

  • Purposeful and connected to important learning
  • Difficult but still achievable
  • Supported through prompts, feedback and prior knowledge
  • Followed by explanation, reflection or consolidation

It is not

  • Leaving pupils confused without support
  • Using difficulty for its own sake
  • Overloading working memory with unnecessary complexity
  • Withholding essential knowledge or instruction

Useful teacher prompts

What have you tried so far?

Which part of the problem do you understand?

What previous learning might help?

What is one smaller step you could take?

Challenge strategy

Removing the Thinking Ceiling

Tasks can unintentionally limit pupils when every step, method or expected answer is predetermined. Removing the ceiling creates room for choice, originality, reasoning and increasingly sophisticated work.

Ways to remove the ceiling

  • Use open questions with several defensible responses
  • Allow pupils to select or compare methods
  • Ask for generalisations, exceptions and counterexamples
  • Invite pupils to improve, adapt or critique a model
  • Require conclusions to be justified with evidence

Replace this

Closed: Complete the next five questions.

More open: Which example best demonstrates the principle? Justify your choice and create an exception.

Planning check

Ask whether your most confident pupils can finish the task without making any meaningful decisions. If they can, the task may have a low thinking ceiling.

Improving the Level of Challenge in a Task

Practical ways to adapt existing classroom activities.

Task adaptation

Require Justification

Increase the challenge of a task by requiring pupils to explain how they know, support claims with evidence and defend their decisions.

Ways to adapt a task

  • Ask pupils to explain every important step
  • Require evidence for conclusions
  • Ask pupils to anticipate objections
  • Request a proof, counterexample or supporting example

Useful prompts

What evidence supports your conclusion?

Why does this method work?

How would you convince someone who disagrees?

Under what conditions would your answer not be true?

Task adaptation

Introduce Constraints

Carefully chosen constraints can make a familiar task more demanding by requiring pupils to make decisions, prioritise information and think strategically.

Possible constraints

  • Limit the tools, methods or sources pupils may use
  • Set a word, time or resource limit
  • Require two contrasting solutions
  • Specify an audience, purpose or context
  • Add a condition that rules out the obvious response

Example

Instead of “Solve the problem”, try: “Solve it in two ways, but do not use the method demonstrated earlier. Which method is more efficient?”

Evaluative thinking

Compare and Evaluate

Asking pupils to compare alternatives and make a justified judgement often creates greater challenge without requiring additional content.

Ways to adapt a task

  • Compare two methods, texts, explanations or models
  • Rank possible responses using explicit criteria
  • Identify strengths, weaknesses and limitations
  • Select the most effective approach and justify the choice

Useful prompts

Which approach is most effective, and according to what criteria?

What does one example reveal that the other does not?

Which response is most convincing, and why?

How could the weaker response be improved?

Independent thinking

Increase Independence

A task becomes more challenging when pupils must make meaningful decisions about how to begin, which strategy to use and how to judge the quality of their work.

Ways to reduce unnecessary support

  • Remove step-by-step instructions once they are no longer needed
  • Offer prompts rather than complete solutions
  • Allow pupils to select methods and resources
  • Ask pupils to plan before beginning
  • Use checklists that support self-monitoring

Useful prompts

What is your plan?

Which strategy will you choose, and why?

How will you check whether your approach is working?

What could you try before asking for help?

Research background

Cognitive Science and Challenge

Cognitive science can help teachers distinguish productive intellectual demand from unnecessary cognitive overload. Challenge should require meaningful thinking while remaining connected to secure knowledge and appropriate support.

Key ideas to explore

  • Working-memory limitations
  • Prior knowledge and schema development
  • Retrieval and purposeful practice
  • Managing intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load
  • Moving gradually towards independent performance

Do pupils possess the prior knowledge needed for the task?

Which support will enable thinking without completing it for them?
